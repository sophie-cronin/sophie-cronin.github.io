Dating back to the 1500s, the fleuron is one of the oldest instances of ornamental type. The term refers to a glyph in the form of plantlife, such as flowers, used for decoration. In fact, you've probably already seen one: ❦ !

They sometimes served a purpose other than pure decoration, such as use as bullet points, punctuation marks, or paragraph dividers. Whatever their purpose was, these historical remnants of the printing press era remind us that type and design as a whole is never too far removed from nature.

On that note, I was intrigued by one of the newest releases of niche fragrance house Liis, named Flower Glyph. With notes like "iridescent ink" and "pollen," the perfume draws inspiration from the fleurons explored here and asks: what other ways can we experience the crossover of type and nature? Through this connection, Liis claims, flowers become language.

This site is a small experiment to explore randomization as well as typographic representations of scents - to me, bergamot is not unlike a green plumbob suspended above a composition. Giavaudan's patented Akigalawood is not unlike a thick censor line to signal proprietary processes and trade secrets, calling upon the issues of ownership and exclusivity in perfumery.

Play around and find a typographic mark that speaks to you!
